Endless browsing: why people open fashion apps without intending to buy

Fashion interfaces are built for the visitor who came to purchase. Most sessions are not that. This study asks what those visitors are actually doing — and rebuilds the feed around the answer.

§ 01

A session that ends without a purchase is treated as a failure. Is it one?

I kept noticing the same behaviour in myself and in people around me: opening a shopping app with no plan, scrolling for ten or fifteen minutes, saving something, closing it. In the shop's analytics that session is a bounce. From inside the session it was successful — the person got what they came for, which was ideas.

If most sessions are not shopping sessions, then the interface is optimised for the minority of visits.

Research questions

  1. RQ1What motivates people to open a fashion app with no purchase intention?
  2. RQ2What do they actually do during those sessions?
  3. RQ3What stops them when they do find something they like?
  4. RQ4What brings them back, and what would make the return visit easier?
ScopeA small qualitative study. It exists to surface patterns and generate design hypotheses — not to measure how common those patterns are. No claim of statistical representativeness is made anywhere in this paper.
§ 02

Method — eight semi-structured retrospective interviews, designed, not yet run

Retrospective rather than hypothetical: each interview starts from the participant's most recent real session. People invent plausible reasons for hypothetical behaviour and are much worse at inventing them for something they did last Tuesday.

Screener. Opened a fashion shopping app at least twice in the past month, and at least once with no specific item in mind. Mixed ages, mixed shopping frequency.

Ethics, as written into the protocol. Verbal consent, no personal data stored, audio only where agreed, free to stop at any point, participant IDs only. This is the procedure the sessions will follow — they have not been held yet.

▸ The full interview guide (11 questions)
  1. Tell me about the last time you opened a fashion shopping app without planning to buy.
  2. Why did you open the app that time?
  3. What did you browse during that session?
  4. Roughly how long did you browse?
  5. Did you find anything you liked?
  6. If you liked something, what stopped you from buying it?
  7. Did you compare the item with other options? How?
  8. Did you save or wishlist anything? What happened to it afterwards?
  9. What usually makes you return to the app?
  10. What would make browsing more enjoyable or easier?
  11. What information would help you decide whether to buy later?

Probes: “why”, “show me”, “what did you expect to happen”. Leading probes (“wouldn’t it be better if…”) were deliberately avoided.

Analysis1. Write up immediately. 2. Break each transcript into one observation per note. 3. Shuffle and cluster bottom-up. 4. Name each cluster after the behaviour. 5. Force every cluster to yield a design implication. 6. Check the implication back against the notes.

Step 5 is the difference between a page of quotes and a piece of research.

§ 06

Journey — where an intent-free session actually breaks

The shape of the curve is the finding. Interest rises through stages 1–3 — the feed is doing its job. Everything collapses at Evaluate and Compare, the two stages where the product stops helping and hands the work back to the user.

§ 07

Direction — five principles, three concepts

  1. P1Serve the session that is happening. Let the person declare a mood; adapt ranking to it.
  2. P2A save must carry its reason. Storage without context is what makes wishlists dead ends.
  3. P3Answer the fit question before it is asked.
  4. P4Never lose the thing being compared.
  5. P5Make waiting legitimate. “Not now” is a valid answer.

C1 · Intent chips → P1. One question at the top of the feed that re-ranks without removing anything. Chips rather than filters because participants read disappearing stock as the shop hiding things.

C2 · Save with a reason → P2, P5. One optional tap files the item under price, fit, occasion or comparison; the saved list groups by the answer.

C3 · Fit confidence → P3. The share of buyers with comparable measurements who kept their usual size, on the card rather than buried in a chart.

Known problemC3 depends on review data most shops do not have, requires body measurements, and a single percentage invites over-interpretation. §10 returns to this — it is the concept I am least comfortable with.

§ 09

Evaluation — moderated usability testing

Goal. Can people complete a browsing session that ends in a useful save — and does the saved list still make sense a week later?

Tasks. (1) Twenty minutes, nothing you need to buy — explore. (2) A wedding next month: find two options and keep them somewhere you will find them again. (3) You like this dress but are unsure of the size — what next? (4) A week later: return to your saved items and explain why each one is there.

Measures. Completion and time on task · errors and hesitation · unaided recall of the save reason in task 4 · Single Ease Question after each task · SUS at the end.

§ 10

Reflection

  • Observe, do not only ask. Retrospective interviews capture the story people tell about a session. A think-aloud with the app open would catch what they never mention.
  • Recruit outside my own circle. Convenience sampling is exactly the bias a study like this needs to avoid.
  • Test the return visit properly. The central claim needs a diary study over two or three weeks, not one follow-up session.
  • Check the fit signal for harm before building it. A confidence number built on reviewers' body data raises privacy and body-image questions I designed past rather than through.

My design training taught me to make things look resolved. This was the first project where the valuable part was the opposite — staying with an unresolved question long enough that the interface followed from evidence rather than taste. The prototype is the least important artefact here; the theme-to-implication mapping in §4 is the part I would defend in a review.
